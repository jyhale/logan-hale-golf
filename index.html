What this season is for

Denver does not need to find anything it does not already have. Hale is at 71.67 a round across nine. Farbu was in the counting five for the first time at Ron Moore and was the second scorer, 211 to Hale’s 209. Hamilton and Capizzi have played only as individuals, and at Ron Moore both finished ahead of two of the five who counted. The 113 was set without Louw or Richani, who were not on that roster; Farbu was, at 75.36 a round, and she is a stroke and a half better than that now. The gains are already on the card.

What sits in front of them is unusually well shaped. Denver's 860 at Red Sky came against a field with fifteen of its twenty teams ranked above Denver, without Farbu counting and with Hamilton at home. A top-50 season — which is where the at-large cut fell in 2026 — would be the program's best since 2023–24.

The risk is depth. Five play and the best four count, so one bad round a day is free — Denver used that at Red Sky, dropping an 80 on the Tuesday. What the format does not cover is two at once, or a lineup that cannot be filled. That question is live every week. February is only where a wrong answer costs the most: the Wallace Classic is first back after the winter, and it was the deepest field on last season’s schedule.

Those four months are also the season's quietest advantage. October to February is rest after four events in five weeks, a full winter of building with no scorecard due on Friday, and open amateur events for anyone who wants to keep competing — which is also where world-ranking points are available while the college schedule is dark. A team that treats the gap as preparation rather than a pause arrives in February sharper than it left in October.

And the distance to the top of the conference is smaller than the leaderboard said. Pepperdine beat Denver by 28 at Red Sky — 19 with Farbu in the counting five. Spread across four counting scores over three rounds, that is a shot and a half per player per round. One bogey and a half. That is the whole distance between Denver and the front of the West Coast Conference.

The conference, April 23–25

Six teams, one week. The 28 strokes Pepperdine took out of Denver at Red Sky were not evenly spread: 18 came in one round, the other two were three and seven. The title also goes for less than that suggests — Pepperdine won the 2026 championship at 865, one over par, and Denver shot 860 last week. On last season's rankings Denver is fourth of six; on this autumn's scoring they project third, with second through fourth a scramble. The title is a real target, not a long shot.

Winning the conference is the clean route: the champion takes an automatic bid to an NCAA regional and is exempt from everything else, including the record test below.

There is also a floor to protect. Since 2024–25, any team taken at large must finish the regular season .500 or better head-to-head against Division I opponents — only the conference champion is exempt. Denver is 28–16–1 (.636) through three events: 9–3 at Redbird, 10–9 at Red Sky, 9–4–1 at Ron Moore. Every tournament is a head-to-head record as well as a finish, and staying above that line is what keeps the second route open at all.

That second route is the national ranking, near 51st in 2026 against Denver's 113th — and the roster case for closing that gap is set out above. What this schedule adds is the chance to close it: three of the six events still to play are Elite or Strong, which is where ranking points are actually earned. The October ranking is the first read on how much of that 113 still applies.
